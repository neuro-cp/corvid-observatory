# Research notes

## What is already known

- An American crow acoustic profiling study examined caws in alarm, food recruitment, and territorial contexts, reporting acoustic information associated with context and individual identity. It emphasizes that crow caws vary continuously and that identifying individuals in the field is difficult. The authors note evidence against assuming that inflected alarm calls identify a particular predator. [Study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4237024/).
- Yorzinski and Vehrencamp compared American crow responses to model owls and raccoons, recording audio and video and measuring calls and behavior. The two contexts differed in mobbing intensity, which complicates any interpretation based solely on predator category. [Paper](https://academic.oup.com/condor/article/111/1/159/5152484).
- A study of urban crows used playback and measured behavioral responses to crow and jackdaw alarm calls in predator and non-predator contexts. It provides an example of testing receiver behavior rather than declaring a meaning from sound alone. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27796660/).

## What our project can currently establish

The first example is an approximately 20-second recording labeled American crow by its contributor. Its behavioral context is not documented. A waveform or spectrogram shows properties of that recording; neither proves why the bird called or what other birds understood. A comparative test needs multiple independent recording sessions with independently observed events and known observation effort.
